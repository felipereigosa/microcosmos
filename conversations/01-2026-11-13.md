## Why Lacrymaria is a good first subject

The hardest part of this project is turning flat, blurry video from a single camera into 3D motion. In general that's very hard. Lacrymaria lets us cheat:

- **It has a simple shape.** It's a teardrop body, a neck, and a small head. At any moment its pose is mostly one curve (the neck) plus the body's position and angle, so we don't need to rebuild a whole surface.
- **The slide keeps it nearly flat.** Under a coverslip, organisms move mostly side to side. We can track x and y from the video and invent a believable small amount of depth. Where the neck goes out of focus, the blur even hints at how far it moved up or down. (It can't tell us which way, but it's a nice extra later.)
- **The detail can be made up, based on real biology.** The fine detail you want doesn't need to come from the blurry footage. The beating hairs (cilia), the spiral rows they grow in, the inner structures and the see-through look can all be built in Blender from biology references. The footage gives the behaviour and the model gives the sharpness. That's an honest version of the "perfect microscope": real movement with plausible detail.
- **There's good reference material.** Stanford published work in 2024 on how its neck folds and unfolds. That gives us real structure to model from instead of guessing.

## The pipeline

1. **Pick a clip:** 5 to 15 seconds, one shot, the organism mostly in focus, little zooming. These videos often pan the slide, so we'd steady the shot against the background first.
2. **Track it:** a video segmentation model (something like SAM 2) cuts out the organism in every frame. Then we take the outline, trace the neck's centre line, and fit a smooth curve to it.
3. **Build the model in Blender:** a tube that follows that curve, with a teardrop body and a head. The neck's length and the body's pose are keyed to the tracked data.
4. **Add the detail:** thousands of cilia beating in waves, a translucent material, and inner structures.
5. **Render:** in Cycles, sharp everywhere, from any angle.

## What we need to test, riskiest first

1. **Does a good clip exist?** We need one long, in-focus stretch without cuts. This only costs us watching videos, but nothing else works without it.
2. **Can we trace the neck reliably?** This is the biggest technical risk. When fully stretched, the neck may be only a few pixels wide and faint. 4. **Does the look work, and how long does it take?** Cilia, translucency, render times. On your laptop's RTX 3060, I'd guess somewhere between 30 seconds and a couple of minutes per frame, so a 10-second clip renders overnight.

## Copying the behaviour

Generally yes, with the caveat that I'm not a lawyer:

- **Behaviour isn't protected.** Copyright covers how footage is made: the framing, the lighting, the editing, the narration. It doesn't cover facts or natural events. How a real Lacrymaria moved its neck is a fact about nature that the camera recorded. The channel didn't create it. For a personal project there's essentially no risk. If it turns into something public, credit them and ask.
