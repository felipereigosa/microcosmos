**The model**
- **Cilia:** about 7,800 on one cell and 6,300 on the other, in 96 rows, each about 10 µm long. The beat is three-dimensional: a straight power stroke to the rear and right, then a low recovery stroke with a bend running out to the tip. They are phased in waves that wind round the body, so the outline shows travelling tufts.
- **Other cilia:** a tuft of longer ones at the back, and those in the oral groove beat towards the mouth.
- **Inside:** macronucleus and micronucleus, two contractile vacuoles with radiating canals that beat in turn, the gullet, and food vacuoles that form at the gullet, travel round and are digested.
- **Close up:** the pellicle's lattice, trichocysts, basal bodies, mitochondria and Golgi fade in, as on the Lacrymaria.

**The motion**
- **Video:** I used two CC BY clips of *P. caudatum* by Alexander Klepnev on Wikimedia Commons. In one, the cell grazes along a floc: it keeps its front end in the debris and swings its body through about 100 degrees and back every 7 seconds or so, with short jerks backwards. In the other it noses a crumb, then darts out of frame in under a second.
- **In the scene:** each cell goes round three flocs. It grazes at each, then backs up, swings its front round with the back end still, and darts to the next at up to 900 µm/s, turning over to the left on a slight spiral. The cilia speed up and reverse their stroke when it backs up.
- **Loop and clearances:** the loop still closes. A checker confirms neither cell passes through the Lacrymaria, the algae, the thick filaments or the other cell.

**Sources**
- The cilia numbers, swimming speed, spiral and avoiding reaction come from an open-access review (Brette, eNeuro 2021).
- The outline and the grazing come from the videos.
- The organelles are textbook cell biology placed by hand.
- The food vacuoles cycle far faster than in life.

## Claude

Yes to both, within limits. These figures are from memory, not looked up today:

- **Small paramecia:** *P. caudatum*, the one I built, is the big one (170 to 300 µm). The *P. aurelia* group runs about 80 to 170 µm, and *P. bursaria* (the green one, full of symbiotic algae) and *P. putrinum* about 80 to 150 µm.
- **Big Lacrymaria:** the body is usually given as about 100 µm when contracted. Ours is 59 µm, measured from the footage, and the Science paper says about 40 µm, so 100 µm is the top of the range.

So the most the real sizes allow is a Lacrymaria body of about 100 µm next to a paramecium of about 80 to 90 µm: a little bigger in the body, and much longer once the neck is out.

What I would do:

1. **Lacrymaria and algae:** leave them exactly as they are on screen and call the Lacrymaria a 100 µm individual. That makes the whole drop 1.7 times bigger in real terms.
2. **Paramecia:** shrink both to about half their current size on screen, so they come out at about 85 and 75 µm real, a bit smaller than the Lacrymaria's body. The cilia would get relatively longer on the smaller cell, as they should, since cilia stay about 10 µm whatever the cell size.
3. **Species:** relabel it as a small one from the *P. aurelia* group. For that I would round off the pointed back end and give it two small micronuclei instead of one. The grazing and darting stay as they are.

One thing does not fit: at that scale the alga's cells come out at about 30 µm, and the Desmodesmus paper tops out at 23. Keeping them the same size relative to the Lacrymaria, as you asked, means accepting that or shrinking them by about a quarter.

Shall I go ahead like that, and should the algae stay as they are or shrink to fit the paper?
